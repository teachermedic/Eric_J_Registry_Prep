How cellular energy connects to a struggling heart.

Follow energy from a heart muscle cell to organ function and patient findings. This is one contributing pathway: heart failure also involves pressure, structure, valves, and other causes.

Stepping stone 1 of 6Stepping stone 1 of 6

Mitochondria

Meet the cell’s energy machinery.

EMT · Recognize

Mitochondria are structures inside cells that help turn nutrients and oxygen into usable energy.

AEMT · Connect

Heart muscle works continuously, so its cells contain many mitochondria.

Paramedic · Explain

Oxidative metabolism supplies most cardiac ATP. Mitochondria also participate in calcium regulation and cellular signaling.

Evidence: [1]

Why do heart muscle cells need a steady energy supply?

Explain the connection

A beating heart repeatedly spends energy, even at rest.

Stepping stone 2 of 6Stepping stone 2 of 6

ATP

Energy supports both squeeze and release.

EMT · Recognize

ATP is usable cellular energy. Heart muscle needs it to contract and relax.

AEMT · Connect

Energy also powers pumps that move calcium, helping each heartbeat reset.

Paramedic · Explain

ATP supports cross-bridge cycling and calcium reuptake into the sarcoplasmic reticulum; relaxation requires energy too.

Evidence: [1]

Which part of a heartbeat needs energy?

Explain the connection

Relaxation is an active process, not just stopping contraction.

Stepping stone 3 of 6Stepping stone 3 of 6

Cellular stress

Energy is part of a larger system.

EMT · Recognize

Disrupted cellular energy can contribute to heart muscle that pumps or fills poorly.

AEMT · Connect

Energy supply, calcium handling, and oxidative stress can become disrupted together.

Paramedic · Explain

Mitochondrial dysfunction can promote maladaptive signaling and cell injury. Heart failure is more complex than simply running out of ATP.

Evidence: [1]

Does mitochondrial dysfunction explain every case of heart failure?

Explain the connection

A useful connection is not a universal explanation.

Stepping stone 4 of 6Stepping stone 4 of 6

Heart function

A problem with pumping, filling, or both.

EMT · Recognize

A heart may be too weak to pump well or too stiff to fill properly.

AEMT · Connect

Heart failure can involve impaired contraction or impaired filling, even when ejection fraction is preserved.

Paramedic · Explain

Reduced ejection fraction and preserved ejection fraction describe different patterns; both can involve elevated filling pressures and inadequate effective circulation.

Evidence: [2], [3], [4]

Can a patient have heart failure with preserved ejection fraction?

Explain the connection

A preserved proportion ejected does not guarantee normal filling or pressures.

Stepping stone 5 of 6Stepping stone 5 of 6

Two branches

Think flow AND pressure.

EMT · Recognize

Reduced effective circulation can contribute to fatigue. Fluid congestion can make breathing difficult.

AEMT · Connect

Left-sided congestion can raise pressure in the lung circulation, while systemic congestion can cause swelling.

Paramedic · Explain

Elevated pulmonary venous pressure can favor fluid movement into lung tissue. Congestion and reduced forward flow may coexist.

Evidence: [2], [3], [4]

Which branch helps explain breathlessness with lung congestion?

Explain the connection

Trace congestion through pressures, rather than assuming all symptoms come from low output.

Stepping stone 6 of 6Stepping stone 6 of 6

Patient findings

Bring the science back to assessment.

EMT · Recognize

Heart failure can cause breathlessness, trouble lying flat, or swelling. Assess breathing and perfusion and arrange care.

AEMT · Connect

Compare breathing effort, oxygenation, perfusion, and changes over time. Look for other possible causes.

Paramedic · Explain

Integrate the clinical picture rather than assigning a molecular cause from symptoms. Assess oxygenation, ventilation, perfusion, and reassessment trends.

Evidence: [2], [3], [4]

Do extra pillows and breathlessness establish the cellular cause?

Why can CPAP help pulmonary edema yet worsen a hypotensive circulation?

Explain the connection

Patient findings support a differential; they do not reveal mitochondrial function.

Positive intrathoracic pressure changes cardiac loading in two directions. Reassess oxygenation and perfusion rather than assuming respiratory improvement guarantees hemodynamic tolerance.

Treatment: connect the why

Prehospital actions require authorization. Definitive and long-term care are included to explain physiology, not to expand field scope.

EMT · Recognize

Recognize breathlessness, difficulty lying flat, and possible poor perfusion. Support ABCs, provide indicated oxygen and protocol-authorized care, and arrange transport. Symptoms do not reveal mitochondrial function.

Evidence: [5], [6]

AEMT · Connect

Connect impaired pumping or filling to congestion and circulation. Reassess breathing effort, oxygenation, and perfusion after support. Positive-pressure support can help selected patients but requires attention to blood pressure and contraindications.

Evidence: [5], [6]

Paramedic · Explain

Prehospital: distinguish hypertensive pulmonary edema from hypotensive pump failure. CPAP can recruit lung units and reduce left-ventricular transmural pressure, improving oxygenation and reducing afterload. It also reduces venous return; reassess for hypotension and inadequate perfusion. Protocol-directed nitrates can reduce cardiac loading in appropriately selected patients, but vasodilation may worsen an already preload-dependent or hypotensive circulation.

Definitive care: acute congestion may involve fluid redistribution as well as excess total volume. Diuresis is appropriate when volume overload contributes, with monitoring of kidney function and electrolytes. Treat ischemia, dysrhythmia, or other triggers when present. Oxygenation, respiratory effort, blood pressure, mental status, and treatment response should guide repeated assessment. A patient with pulmonary edema and shock needs a different strategy from one with severe hypertension. These therapies improve loading conditions and organ perfusion; they are not direct evidence that mitochondrial ATP production has recovered.

Evidence: [5], [6]

Keep the whole picture

Heart failure can occur with reduced or preserved ejection fraction. Symptoms do not identify mitochondrial dysfunction in an individual patient.
