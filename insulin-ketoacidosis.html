From cell to patient

Insulin → Diabetic Ketoacidosis

Two connected branches: ketones and water loss.

Connect insufficient insulin action to excess ketones and dehydration. Follow both branches to understand why blood glucose alone does not explain the whole emergency.

Start with this patient

A patient with diabetes has vomiting, thirst, frequent urination, and unusually deep breathing. A glucose reading is one clue; which other mechanisms could explain the presentation?

Stepping stone 1 of 6

Insulin

A signal that helps regulate fuel use.

EMT · Recognize

Insulin helps the body use glucose and regulates how fuel is handled.

AEMT · Connect

Insulin supports glucose uptake in muscle and fat and helps restrain excess glucose production.

Paramedic · Explain

Insulin regulates substrate use; its actions extend beyond glucose entry into insulin-sensitive tissues.

Is insulin only relevant to a number on the meter?

Explain the connection

Start with the hormone’s function, not just the measurement.

Stepping stone 2 of 6

Insufficient action

The fuel balance shifts.

EMT · Recognize

Too little effective insulin can lead to high blood glucose and increased fat use.

AEMT · Connect

Illness or interrupted insulin delivery can precipitate this imbalance.

Paramedic · Explain

Insulin deficiency and increased counterregulatory hormones promote hepatic glucose production and lipolysis.

Which situation can help trigger DKA?

Explain the connection

Ask about illness, medications, and insulin delivery.

Stepping stone 3 of 6

Ketones

Follow the acid branch.

EMT · Recognize

Fat breakdown can produce excess ketones. Their accumulation contributes to an acidic blood chemistry.

AEMT · Connect

Ketone production links the fuel problem to metabolic acidosis.

Paramedic · Explain

Hepatic ketogenesis increases; ketonemia and metabolic acidosis distinguish DKA from uncomplicated hyperglycemia.

Fat / ketone branch
Excess ketones → metabolic acidosis

Glucose / kidney branch
Glucose loss in urine → water and electrolyte loss

What connects fat breakdown to DKA’s acid problem?

Explain the connection

Ketoacidosis involves ketones and acid-base disturbance.

Stepping stone 4 of 6

Water loss

Follow the glucose branch.

EMT · Recognize

High blood glucose can cause glucose and water loss in urine, contributing to dehydration.

AEMT · Connect

Frequent urination and thirst can reflect this water loss.

Paramedic · Explain

Glycosuria causes osmotic diuresis with water and electrolyte losses; serum potassium may not reflect total-body stores.

How can high glucose contribute to dehydration?

Explain the connection

Trace glucose through the kidney to fluid loss.

Stepping stone 5 of 6

Patient findings

Both branches reach the bedside.

EMT · Recognize

Vomiting, thirst, and fast, deep breathing can occur in DKA.

AEMT · Connect

Deep breathing can be a response to metabolic acidosis; assess it in the whole clinical picture.

Paramedic · Explain

Respiratory compensation increases carbon dioxide elimination. Acidosis and volume depletion can coexist.

Why might a patient with DKA breathe deeply?

Explain the connection

Do not automatically label every breathing change as a primary lung problem.

Stepping stone 6 of 6

Reasoning

A meter reading is one part of the story.

EMT · Recognize

Assess breathing, mental status, perfusion, and glucose. The meter alone does not confirm DKA.

AEMT · Connect

Consider the history and patient findings; laboratory assessment establishes ketones and acid-base status.

Paramedic · Explain

Euglycemic DKA is possible. Diagnosis requires the appropriate diabetes/glucose context plus ketosis and metabolic acidosis.

Does a glucose reading alone confirm or exclude DKA?

Explain the connection

Follow both branches instead of stopping at the glucose value.

Keep the whole picture

DKA can occur without markedly elevated glucose, including with SGLT2 inhibitor use. Confirmation requires ketone and acid-base assessment, not a glucose reading alone.
