Start with the whole patient.

The heart in the thorax

EMT field notes

The heart sits between the lungs, behind the sternum and above the diaphragm. Its apex points toward the patient’s left. The ribs are drawn translucent here so you can see the organs beneath them.

AEMT field notes

The heart occupies the middle mediastinum within the pericardial sac. The left lung has a cardiac notch. Keep patient right and left distinct from your right and left when viewing the chest from the front.

Paramedic field notes

Anatomical position helps you orient examination findings, but a location alone does not establish a diagnosis. Use this view to connect the chest, circulation and ventilation before following a disease mechanism.

Two circuits. One coordinated pump.

Inside the heart

EMT field notes

The right heart sends blood to the lungs. The left heart sends blood to the body. Atria receive blood; ventricles eject it. Follow the arrows through the four chambers in this simplified cutaway.

AEMT field notes

The septum separates the right and left sides. Valves support one-way flow. The left ventricular wall is thicker because it works against the higher resistance of the systemic circulation.

Paramedic field notes

Cardiac output depends on heart rate and stroke volume. Filling, contractility and the pressure against which a ventricle ejects all influence stroke volume. Next, inspect the myocardium that generates this force.

Separate cells working together.

Cardiac muscle tissue

EMT field notes

Cardiac muscle is made of branching cells. Their repeating contractile units create stripes called striations. Specialized connections help the cells stay joined and coordinate a heartbeat.

AEMT field notes

Intercalated discs contain mechanical junctions and gap junctions. Mechanical junctions resist separation during contraction; gap junctions allow electrical current to pass between adjacent cells.

Paramedic field notes

Electrical coupling lets working myocardium behave as a coordinated network. Conduction and force generation are related but distinct: an electrical rhythm alone does not demonstrate effective mechanical output.

Further reading: [2]

Energy, calcium and contraction.

A single cardiomyocyte

EMT field notes

A cardiomyocyte is one cardiac muscle cell. Its contractile machinery needs energy. Mitochondria help supply ATP; calcium helps switch contraction on. The central nucleus contains genetic information.

AEMT field notes

An electrical signal activates calcium entry and calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium enables contraction. Relaxation requires calcium removal from the cytosol as well as detachment of the contractile filaments.

Paramedic field notes

L-type calcium entry triggers calcium release through RyR2 channels. Calcium binds troponin C, permitting cross-bridge cycling. SERCA uses ATP to return calcium to the sarcoplasmic reticulum; the sodium–calcium exchanger also contributes to calcium removal. Disturbed cycling can impair both contraction and relaxation.

Further reading: [3], [4]

Instructions for maintaining the cell.

Inside the nucleus

EMT field notes

The nucleus holds DNA. The nucleolus helps assemble the machinery needed to make proteins. This is the final stage of the explorer; return to the cell to put these structures in context.

AEMT field notes

Chromatin is DNA associated with proteins. Nuclear pores regulate traffic across the nuclear envelope. RNA messages carry information toward protein synthesis in the cytoplasm.

Paramedic field notes

Changes in gene expression can change the amounts of contractile proteins, channels and other cellular components over time. Gene expression is not the immediate trigger for each heartbeat; excitation–contraction coupling operates on a much faster timescale.
