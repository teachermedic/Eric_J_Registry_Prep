Cytoplasm & Cytosol → Sepsis

Connect cellular metabolism, lactate, and failing organ function.

Cytoplasm includes the cytosol and structures outside the nucleus. Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol. Sepsis can disrupt metabolism and perfusion, but an elevated lactate is not proof that the cytoplasm has been destroyed.

Start with this patient

A patient with a suspected infection is newly confused, tachypneic, and poorly perfused. Use the pattern to consider sepsis rather than waiting for any single number to confirm it.

Metabolism in the cytosol

Metabolism in the cytosol

EMT · Recognize

The cytosol is fluid inside the cell where glycolysis begins breaking down glucose for energy.

AEMT · Connect

Cytoplasm includes cytosol and organelles outside the nucleus. Glycolysis in cytosol produces pyruvate; subsequent metabolism depends on cellular conditions.

Paramedic · Explain

Lactate dehydrogenase interconverts pyruvate and lactate while coupling NADH/NAD+ balance. Lactate production is compatible with oxygen availability and can participate in normal fuel exchange.

Stepping stone 2 of 6

Infection provokes a systemic response

Infection provokes a systemic response

EMT · Recognize

Infection can trigger a harmful body-wide response.

AEMT · Connect

In sepsis, a dysregulated host response can impair organs. Vascular tone, permeability, and microcirculatory flow may all change.

Paramedic · Explain

Inflammatory and endothelial dysfunction interact with altered cellular energetics. Macrohemodynamic improvement does not guarantee uniform restoration of microvascular flow or oxygen use.

Stepping stone 3 of 6

Stress changes metabolism and perfusion

Stress changes metabolism and perfusion

EMT · Recognize

Sepsis can disrupt circulation and cell metabolism, contributing to organ dysfunction.

AEMT · Connect

Lactate may rise from impaired oxygen delivery, accelerated glycolysis during stress, or reduced clearance. An elevated value does not prove one mechanism.

Paramedic · Explain

Catecholamine-driven aerobic glycolysis can increase lactate production, while liver dysfunction can reduce clearance. Interpret lactate alongside perfusion and trajectory rather than equating every elevation with anaerobic cell death.

Evidence: [1], [2]

Does high lactate always prove oxygen-starved cells?

Explain the connection

Lactate is useful context, not a unique molecular explanation.

Stepping stone 4 of 6

Circulatory dysfunction

Circulatory dysfunction

EMT · Recognize

Low pressure or uneven flow can reduce delivery to organs.

AEMT · Connect

Vasodilation and vascular leak may lower effective filling and arterial pressure. Some patients also develop myocardial dysfunction.

Paramedic · Explain

Reduced vascular resistance, altered venous return, and cardiac dysfunction can coexist. A restored arterial pressure is a useful target but does not, by itself, confirm adequate tissue-level perfusion.

Evidence: [3], [4]

Explain the connection

Stepping stone 5 of 6

Look for organ dysfunction

Look for organ dysfunction

EMT · Recognize

New confusion, fast breathing, and poor perfusion during infection are concerning.

AEMT · Connect

Assess respiratory status, mental status, circulation, temperature, and the suspected source. Older or immunocompromised patients may not have a high fever.

Paramedic · Explain

Integrate the history with trends in vital signs, capillary refill, mental status, and available testing. No isolated screening score or lactate result should replace clinical assessment.

Evidence: [4], [5]

Which finding matters even without fever?

Explain the connection

Sepsis can present without a classic high fever.

Stepping stone 6 of 6

Patient findings → restore perfusion

Patient findings → restore perfusion

EMT · Recognize

Infection with new confusion, fast breathing, or poor perfusion needs urgent assessment, support, and transport.

AEMT · Connect

Support oxygenation and protocol-directed circulation care. Communicate the suspected infection and organ dysfunction early to the receiving team.

Paramedic · Explain

Treat the physiology and the infection together. Reassess fluid response and consider authorized vasopressor support for persistent hypotension; hospital antibiotics and source control address the trigger.

Evidence: [4], [5]

Treatment: connect the why

Prehospital actions require authorization. Definitive and long-term care are included to explain physiology, not to expand field scope.

EMT · Recognize

Support ABCs, identify suspected infection plus new organ dysfunction, and arrange prompt transport. Reassess mental status, breathing, and perfusion, and provide an early receiving-team report.

Evidence: [1], [4], [5]

AEMT · Connect

Vascular leak and vasodilation can compromise effective circulation. Use protocol-directed fluids and reassess their effect rather than treating tachycardia alone. Support oxygenation and monitor for worsening respiratory findings.

Evidence: [1], [4], [5]

Paramedic · Explain

Prehospital: assess likely infection, organ dysfunction, and hemodynamic response. Crystalloid can improve filling and venous return, but capillary leak or cardiac dysfunction can limit benefit and increase congestion. Reassess after fluid using perfusion findings and authorized response measures. Persistent hypotension may require protocol-directed vasopressor support; unstable shock may require concurrent support rather than prolonged fluid-only treatment.

Norepinephrine generally restores pressure by increasing vascular tone, but pressure recovery does not guarantee normal microcirculation. Interpret lactate trends with mental status, capillary refill, urine output when available, and respiratory status. Do not repeatedly add fluid solely to normalize lactate.

Definitive care: timely antimicrobials and source control address the infection; give antimicrobials in the field only where specifically authorized. The 2026 guideline generally recommends norepinephrine first line, with individualized choices for concomitant cardiac dysfunction. Continued management balances perfusion, oxygen delivery, organ function, and adverse effects. There is no routine EMS therapy that directly measures or repairs cytosolic metabolism.

Evidence: [1], [4], [5]

Keep the whole picture

Lactate can rise through increased production or reduced clearance, with or without severe tissue hypoxia. Sepsis is a clinical syndrome of infection-associated organ dysfunction, not a lactate diagnosis or a direct measure of cellular destruction.
