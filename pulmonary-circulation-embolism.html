Pulmonary Circulation → Pulmonary Embolism

From an obstructed lung vessel to right-heart strain and shock.

The right ventricle normally sends blood through a low-resistance pulmonary circulation. A pulmonary embolism obstructs that route and can disturb gas exchange, right-heart loading, and systemic circulation.

Start with this patient

A patient develops sudden breathlessness and pleuritic chest discomfort after prolonged immobility. They are tachycardic and nearly faint on standing. Consider PE while evaluating other serious causes.

Where would you like to start?

EMT: three simple stops. AEMT: six mechanism connections. Paramedic: deeper physiology and treatment reasoning. Learning views do not expand clinical scope; care follows local protocols and medical direction.

Low-resistance lung circulation

Low-resistance lung circulation

EMT · Recognize

The right ventricle pumps blood through the lungs for gas exchange.

AEMT · Connect

The pulmonary circulation normally accepts flow at lower pressures than the systemic circulation. Right-heart function depends on that loading environment.

Paramedic · Explain

The relatively thin-walled RV is adapted to a low-resistance vascular bed. Abrupt afterload increases differ from gradual pressure loading with time for structural adaptation.

An embolus blocks vessels

An embolus blocks vessels

EMT · Recognize

A clot can travel to a pulmonary artery and obstruct blood flow.

AEMT · Connect

Venous thrombi can embolize to the lungs. Obstructed vessels reduce perfusion to some ventilated regions and increase resistance to RV ejection.

Paramedic · Explain

Mechanical obstruction and vascular responses raise pulmonary vascular resistance. Consequences depend on clot distribution and cardiopulmonary reserve, not clot size alone.

Evidence: [1], [2]

Does the effect depend only on clot size?

Explain the connection

A patient's reserve changes the response to an obstruction.

Obstruction → gas-exchange and pump problems

Obstruction → gas-exchange and pump problems

EMT · Recognize

Blocked lung circulation can cause breathlessness and strain the right heart, sometimes leading to shock.

AEMT · Connect

Some air reaches lung units with inadequate blood flow. Meanwhile, the RV has to pump against increased resistance.

Paramedic · Explain

PE increases alveolar dead space in affected units and can create broader V/Q abnormalities. RV pressure loading can decrease output even when ventilation continues.

Evidence: [1], [2], [3]

Can severe PE be a circulatory problem as well as a breathing problem?

Explain the connection

PE links gas exchange to obstructive circulatory physiology.

Stepping stone 4 of 6Stepping stone 4 of 6

RV strain reduces LV filling

RV strain reduces LV filling

EMT · Recognize

A struggling right heart can reduce blood reaching the left heart.

AEMT · Connect

RV dilation can interfere with LV filling, and reduced forward flow lowers the supply available to the LV. Systemic output may fall.

Paramedic · Explain

Septal displacement and ventricular interdependence compound low LV preload. Systemic hypotension can then impair RV coronary perfusion, worsening the failure cycle.

Evidence: [1], [2], [4]

How can a lung clot reduce systemic output?

Explain the connection

Ventricular loading connects the two sides of the circulation.

Assess shock and differential

Assess shock and differential

EMT · Recognize

Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or fainting needs a serious-cause assessment.

AEMT · Connect

Assess perfusion, mental status, respiratory effort, risk context, and competing diagnoses. Neither a symptom nor a risk factor confirms PE.

Paramedic · Explain

Monitoring and ECG help assess complications but do not exclude PE when normal or nonspecific. Definitive testing and risk assessment guide treatment rather than a single ECG stereotype.

Evidence: [3], [5]

Does absence of a classic PE ECG pattern rule it out?

Explain the connection

ECG contributes to assessment; it is not a definitive exclusion test.

Patient findings → support and escalation

Patient findings → support and escalation

EMT · Recognize

PE may cause sudden breathlessness, chest pain, fainting, or shock. Support ABCs and expedite evaluation.

AEMT · Connect

Support oxygenation and circulation under protocol, reassess, and communicate concern and instability to the receiving team.

Paramedic · Explain

Protect the failing RV while arranging cause-directed care. Fluid, vasopressor, and ventilation decisions require attention to loading conditions and response, not an automatic treatment for every dyspneic patient.

Evidence: [3], [4], [5]

Does better saturation establish resolution of the obstruction?

Why can an automatic large fluid load worsen severe PE with RV failure?

Explain the connection

Oxygenation and pulmonary vascular obstruction are separate targets.

Assess volume status and response. Supporting an underfilled patient is different from overloading an already strained RV.

Treatment: connect the why

Prehospital actions require authorization. Definitive and long-term care are included to explain physiology, not to expand field scope.

EMT · Recognize

Support ABCs, assess perfusion and respiratory status, and arrange prompt transport. Provide indicated oxygen and ventilation support under protocol. Communicate sudden onset, risk context, syncope, and shock findings.

Evidence: [3], [4], [5]

AEMT · Connect

Connect obstruction to both gas exchange and increased RV work. Support circulation within scope and reassess after interventions. Improving oxygenation does not establish normal cardiac output or eliminate the clot.

Evidence: [3], [4], [5]

Paramedic · Explain

Prehospital: in suspected severe PE, support oxygenation and systemic perfusion while anticipating RV failure. Fluid may help a genuinely underfilled circulation, but indiscriminate large volumes can worsen RV dilation, septal shift, and LV filling. Protocol-directed vasopressor support may be needed for shock. Reassess pressure, mental status, rhythm, respiratory status, and response.

Positive-pressure ventilation can reduce venous return and increase RV loading. Needed ventilatory support must not be withheld, but severe RV failure makes sedation and airway transition high risk; prepare hemodynamic support and use authorized strategies that avoid unnecessary pressure. Oxygen improvement alone does not correct vascular obstruction.

Definitive care: anticoagulation limits thrombus propagation and recurrence; it is not immediate mechanical removal of the existing clot. Selected higher-risk patients may need thrombolysis, catheter-based treatment, or surgical embolectomy, balancing hemodynamic benefit against bleeding and procedural risks. Those decisions require diagnostic confirmation and specialist assessment under the current PE pathway. Keep supportive physiology separate from clot-directed therapy.

Evidence: [3], [4], [5]

Keep the whole picture

Symptoms and risk factors cannot confirm PE or determine its severity alone. A normal oxygen saturation or absence of one classic ECG pattern does not exclude it. Collapse can occur through circulatory failure as well as impaired oxygenation.
