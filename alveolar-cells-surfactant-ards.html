Alveolar Cells & Surfactant → ARDS

From barrier injury and unstable alveoli to difficult oxygenation.

Alveolar cells, capillary barriers, and surfactant help maintain gas exchange. In ARDS, inflammatory injury can disrupt the barrier and surfactant function, causing edema, collapse, and poor oxygenation. This differs from primarily pressure-driven cardiogenic edema.

Start with this patient

A patient with a serious infection has worsening respiratory distress and low oxygen despite increasing support. Consider inflammatory lung injury alongside heart failure and other causes of respiratory failure.

Where would you like to start?

EMT: three simple stops. AEMT: six mechanism connections. Paramedic: deeper physiology and treatment reasoning. Learning views do not expand clinical scope; care follows local protocols and medical direction.

Keep alveoli stable and thin

Keep alveoli stable and thin

EMT · Recognize

Alveoli exchange gases. Their lining and surfactant help keep them stable and functioning.

AEMT · Connect

The alveolar–capillary barrier limits fluid leakage, while type II cells produce surfactant that lowers surface tension.

Paramedic · Explain

Type I cells provide much of the thin exchange surface; type II cells support surfactant and repair. Surfactant reduces surface forces that otherwise promote instability and collapse.

Explain the connection

Inflammation injures the barrier

Inflammation injures the barrier

EMT · Recognize

Severe inflammation can damage the lung’s gas-exchange surfaces.

AEMT · Connect

Pneumonia, aspiration, sepsis, and other insults can contribute to alveolar epithelial and endothelial injury. Barrier damage allows abnormal fluid movement.

Paramedic · Explain

Inflammatory injury can increase permeability across the gas-exchange unit. Protein-rich edema and cellular debris alter the alveolar environment, rather than merely reflecting elevated left-heart pressure.

Evidence: [1], [2]

How does inflammatory edema differ from a purely hydrostatic explanation?

Explain the connection

Pressure and permeability are different mechanisms and can coexist.

Leak and dysfunction → poor oxygenation

Leak and dysfunction → poor oxygenation

EMT · Recognize

Leaky barriers and impaired surfactant can leave alveoli flooded or collapsed, making oxygenation difficult.

AEMT · Connect

Loss of aerated lung and reduced compliance increase breathing work. Blood may pass through poorly ventilated regions.

Paramedic · Explain

Protein-rich fluid can inhibit surfactant, while epithelial injury affects its production and handling. These processes interact with inflammation and atelectasis; surfactant dysfunction is one contributor, not a complete explanation.

Evidence: [1], [2]

Is adult ARDS only a shortage of surfactant?

Explain the connection

Adult inflammatory lung injury involves multiple connected structures.

Shunt limits oxygen response

Shunt limits oxygen response

EMT · Recognize

Blood passing poorly ventilated lung units can remain inadequately oxygenated.

AEMT · Connect

Collapsed or flooded units contribute to shunt-like physiology. Added oxygen helps available units but may not fully compensate for the lost exchange areas.

Paramedic · Explain

Perfused but nonventilated regions create intrapulmonary shunt, and injury is heterogeneous. Escalating oxygen concentration alone does not restore ventilation to every affected unit.

Evidence: [2], [4]

Why might oxygen alone have an incomplete effect?

Explain the connection

Support may need to address aeration as well as oxygen concentration.

Support can also injure

Support can also injure

EMT · Recognize

Ventilation support can help, but excessive stretch or pressure can harm injured lungs.

AEMT · Connect

A smaller functioning lung receives the delivered breath. Repeated collapse and reopening and excessive distension can add injury.

Paramedic · Explain

Lung-protective strategies limit tidal stretch and inspiratory pressures. PEEP may maintain aeration, but recruitment benefit competes with overdistension and hemodynamic effects.

Evidence: [3], [4]

Why not simply use ever-larger breaths?

Explain the connection

Rescue gas exchange while limiting additional lung injury.

Treatment: connect the why

Prehospital actions require authorization. Definitive and long-term care are included to explain physiology, not to expand field scope.

EMT · Recognize

Support ABCs and provide indicated oxygen and ventilation support. Assess breathing effort, mental status, and perfusion repeatedly. Communicate infection, aspiration, or other possible triggers and arrange urgent evaluation.

Evidence: [1], [3], [4], [5]

AEMT · Connect

Connect flooding or collapse to lost gas-exchange area and increased work of breathing. Use authorized support and reassess both respiratory and circulatory effects. Low saturation does not identify one specific edema mechanism.

Evidence: [1], [3], [4], [5]

Paramedic · Explain

Prehospital: support gas exchange and assess tolerance of the chosen respiratory support under protocol. Positive pressure may improve aeration but can reduce venous return or cause overdistension; reassess oxygenation, ventilation, work of breathing, and hemodynamics. Avoid treating every crackle as an isolated fluid-overload problem.

Definitive care: lung-protective ventilation limits tidal volume according to predicted body weight and controls inspiratory pressures, aiming to reduce injury to the remaining aerated lung. PEEP can stabilize recruitable units, but higher pressure is not universally better; benefit must be balanced against overdistension and circulation. Prone positioning in selected severe ARDS redistributes ventilation and stress across the lungs; it is a monitored team intervention, not an automatic field maneuver.

The ATS guideline also discusses selected adjuncts according to severity. Treat the precipitating illness and distinguish improved oxygenation from improved survival. Adult surfactant replacement has not demonstrated routine survival benefit, so the trail should not imply that a simple replacement drug repairs all ARDS. Ongoing reassessment balances recruitment, ventilation, perfusion, and treatment-related injury.

Evidence: [1], [3], [4], [5]

Keep the whole picture

ARDS is a syndrome requiring clinical and diagnostic assessment; field crackles or low saturation alone cannot establish it. Cardiogenic and inflammatory mechanisms can coexist. Adult ARDS is not simply neonatal surfactant deficiency.
