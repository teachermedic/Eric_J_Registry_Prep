From cell to patient

Cell Membrane → Rhabdomyolysis

From a muscle cell’s boundary to potassium release and kidney injury.

The cell membrane separates the inside of a cell from its surroundings. Muscle-cell injury can disrupt that boundary and release contents into blood, producing rhabdomyolysis and potentially dangerous systemic effects.

Start with this patient

A patient has been immobilized for a prolonged period after a fall. They report painful swollen muscles and weakness. Think about muscle breakdown even if their urine is not dark.

Where would you like to start?

EMT: three simple stops. AEMT: six mechanism connections. Paramedic: deeper physiology and treatment reasoning. Learning views do not expand clinical scope; care follows local protocols and medical direction.

Maintain a boundary

Maintain a boundary

EMT · Recognize

The cell membrane controls movement between the inside and outside of a muscle cell.

AEMT · Connect

The muscle membrane is called the sarcolemma. Channels and energy-dependent transport maintain different ion concentrations inside and outside.

Paramedic · Explain

Ion gradients support excitability. Na+/K+-ATPase and calcium transport rely directly or indirectly on energy; membrane integrity alone is not enough if transport fails.

Evidence: [1]

What does a healthy muscle membrane help maintain?

Explain the connection

A membrane plus functioning transport helps keep cellular contents controlled.

Injury overwhelms control

Injury overwhelms control

EMT · Recognize

Crushing, heat, or prolonged pressure can damage muscle cells.

AEMT · Connect

Direct injury or energy depletion disrupts ion regulation. Calcium can accumulate inside the cell and activate damaging processes.

Paramedic · Explain

Intracellular calcium overload activates proteases and phospholipases, increasing membrane and organelle injury. Sustained contraction and mitochondrial dysfunction can worsen ATP depletion, reinforcing the cycle.

Evidence: [1], [2]

Why is excess intracellular calcium harmful here?

Explain the connection

Calcium overload can amplify injury rather than simply trigger normal contraction.

Membrane failure → muscle breakdown

Membrane failure → muscle breakdown

EMT · Recognize

Damaged muscle cells leak potassium and proteins into blood. This muscle breakdown is called rhabdomyolysis.

AEMT · Connect

Released potassium can alter cardiac conduction. Myoglobin reaches the kidneys, where hypoperfusion and its effects can contribute to acute kidney injury.

Paramedic · Explain

Necrotic muscle releases electrolytes, myoglobin, and CK. Myoglobin-associated tubular injury and obstruction interact with volume depletion; circulating CK helps detect injury but is not itself the main renal toxin.

Evidence: [2], [3]

What makes muscle breakdown a systemic danger?

Explain the connection

The consequences extend beyond the original muscle injury.

Stepping stone 4 of 6Stepping stone 4 of 6

Potassium changes excitability

Potassium changes excitability

EMT · Recognize

Too much potassium in blood can cause dangerous heart rhythms.

AEMT · Connect

Rising extracellular potassium reduces the gradient across excitable-cell membranes. Severe changes can impair normal conduction.

Paramedic · Explain

Progressive depolarization can inactivate fast sodium channels and slow conduction, producing potentially lethal electrical instability. ECG manifestations vary, so a reassuring tracing does not establish a safe serum potassium.

Evidence: [3], [4]

Does a normal ECG rule out hyperkalemia?

Explain the connection

Use context, monitoring, and hospital electrolyte measurement together.

Kidney risk and perfusion

Kidney risk and perfusion

EMT · Recognize

Weakness, pain, swelling, or dark urine can follow muscle injury; not everyone has all of them.

AEMT · Connect

Fluid sequestration in injured muscle and poor intake can lower circulating volume. Impaired kidney function can further reduce potassium elimination.

Paramedic · Explain

The combination of ongoing potassium release and reduced renal clearance can rapidly magnify risk. Track perfusion, rhythm, and respiratory findings; clinical assessment cannot replace CK, creatinine, and electrolyte testing.

Evidence: [2], [3]

Which statement avoids missing the disease?

Explain the connection

The classic symptom triad is incomplete in many patients.

Patient findings → protect circulation

Patient findings → protect circulation

EMT · Recognize

Muscle breakdown can cause weakness, kidney injury, or dangerous rhythms. Support ABCs and arrange urgent evaluation.

AEMT · Connect

Assess the injury context, perfusion, and rhythm. Fluid and other interventions depend on authorized scope and repeated reassessment.

Paramedic · Explain

Separate three goals: support perfusion, protect the myocardium from hyperkalemia when indicated, and reduce or remove excess potassium. Those actions are not interchangeable.

Evidence: [3], [4]

Does IV calcium remove excess potassium?

The ECG improves after calcium in suspected severe hyperkalemia. What still needs attention?

Explain the connection

Cardiac protection buys time; shifting and removing potassium are different tasks.

Calcium protects the myocardium without reducing the potassium burden. Redistribution is temporary, and removal plus management of ongoing injury may still be needed.

Treatment: connect the why

Prehospital actions require authorization. Definitive and long-term care are included to explain physiology, not to expand field scope.

EMT · Recognize

Support ABCs, identify crush injury or prolonged immobilization, assess perfusion, and expedite transport. Do not wait for dark urine. Escalate care for altered mental status, shock, or rhythm concerns.

Evidence: [3], [4]

AEMT · Connect

Protocol-directed fluids may support renal perfusion and circulating volume. Reassess lungs and perfusion; injured muscle can sequester fluid while kidney dysfunction limits excretion. Obtain monitoring and higher-level support as indicated.

Evidence: [3], [4]

Paramedic · Explain

Prehospital: use the injury context and serial ECG assessment to anticipate hyperkalemia, while recognizing that an ECG cannot measure or exclude it. Give protocol-directed isotonic crystalloid to support perfusion, with reassessment for overload, particularly when renal clearance is impaired.

For suspected hyperkalemia with appropriate clinical or ECG indications, calcium protects cardiac excitability but does not lower potassium. Insulin with glucose shifts potassium into cells by stimulating cellular uptake; glucose surveillance is essential because hypoglycemia can follow. A beta-2 agonist can also shift potassium, but neither approach removes it from the body. Use only therapies authorized by medical direction and local protocols.

Definitive care: repeat electrolytes and renal assessment guide ongoing treatment; selected patients require dialysis for refractory hyperkalemia or other standard indications. Routine bicarbonate or mannitol solely to prevent rhabdomyolysis-associated kidney injury is not supported by the AAST consensus. The clinical target is safe circulation and electrolyte control, not a falsely reassuring improvement in muscle pain.

Evidence: [3], [4]

Keep the whole picture

The classic combination of muscle pain, weakness, and dark urine is not always present. A normal ECG does not exclude hyperkalemia. Rhabdomyolysis requires laboratory evaluation; symptoms alone cannot quantify potassium or kidney injury.
