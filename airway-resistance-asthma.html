Airway Resistance → Asthma

From a narrowing airway to the work of breathing.

Follow airway narrowing into airflow limitation and the patient’s breathing effort. The goal is to explain what you assess, not to diagnose asthma from a single sound.

Start with this patient

A patient with asthma is struggling to speak in full sentences. During reassessment, wheezing becomes quieter but the patient becomes drowsier. Has the patient improved?

Where would you like to start?

EMT: recognize findings. AEMT: connect mechanisms. Paramedic: explain deeper physiology. These are learning views; care follows your local scope and protocols.

Stepping stone 1 of 6

The airway

A pathway for moving air.

EMT · Recognize

Air travels through tubes in the lungs. Open airways allow air to move in and out.

AEMT · Connect

Asthma affects the conducting airways; narrowing can interfere particularly with breathing out.

Paramedic · Explain

Airflow limitation in asthma reflects changes in airway caliber, with variability over time.

What does an airway primarily provide?

Explain the connection

The tube must remain open enough for air movement.

Stepping stone 2 of 6

Narrowing

More than one change can shrink the space.

EMT · Recognize

During an attack, airway tightening and swelling make it harder to move air.

AEMT · Connect

Inflammation, airway muscle contraction, and mucus can contribute to obstruction.

Paramedic · Explain

Bronchoconstriction, mucosal edema, and secretions can act together; a bronchodilator addresses muscle constriction rather than every mechanism.

Which combination can narrow airways?

Explain the connection

Asthma obstruction is not always a single change.

Stepping stone 3 of 6

Resistance

A smaller passage makes airflow harder.

EMT · Recognize

Narrowed airways resist airflow, so breathing requires more effort.

AEMT · Connect

Greater resistance means more pressure is needed to move air through the narrowed passage.

Paramedic · Explain

Airflow limitation increases respiratory workload. A simple tube analogy helps, but real airways are branching and dynamically changing.

What happens when an airway narrows?

Explain the connection

Follow the physical obstruction to the work needed to breathe.

Stepping stone 4 of 6

Exhalation

Getting air out can become difficult.

EMT · Recognize

A patient may have trouble breathing out and may wheeze.

AEMT · Connect

Obstruction can prolong exhalation; the patient may not empty the lungs fully before the next breath.

Paramedic · Explain

Expiratory airflow limitation can cause gas trapping. Consider expiratory time and ventilation as well as oxygen saturation.

Why does the outward part of a breath matter?

Explain the connection

Breathing assessment includes how air leaves the lungs.

Stepping stone 5 of 6

Patient findings

Listen to more than the wheeze.

EMT · Recognize

Look for breathing effort, limited speech, and difficulty breathing, not just a wheezing sound.

AEMT · Connect

Assess air movement and mental status alongside respiratory rate and oxygenation.

Paramedic · Explain

Drowsiness, confusion, or a silent chest can indicate a life-threatening exacerbation. Wheeze intensity alone does not grade severity.

Which finding is especially concerning?

Explain the connection

Reduced airflow and altered mental status require urgent attention.

Stepping stone 6 of 6

Reassessment

Quiet is not automatically better.

EMT · Recognize

Quieter wheezing with worsening alertness is concerning, not reassuring.

AEMT · Connect

Compare the whole assessment before and after care; worsening effort or air movement changes the interpretation.

Paramedic · Explain

Evaluate the trajectory of ventilation and fatigue. A quieter chest with deteriorating mental status may reflect worsening airflow.

In the opening case, what does quieter wheezing prove?

Explain the connection

Interpret the sound in context and reassess the patient.

Keep the whole picture

Less wheezing can mean improvement or dangerously reduced airflow. Interpret sounds alongside air movement, effort, speech, oxygenation, and mental status.
